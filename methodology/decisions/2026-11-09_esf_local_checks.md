# Environmental and social questions at scoping
Version 2.1 design for review, 9 November 2026.

## Purpose and source

M2b asks how hazards could affect the investment during establishment. Section 4.3 adds a qualitative inquiry into how the proposed activity could affect people and nature. Use the already reviewed [World Bank Environmental and Social Framework](https://pubdocs.worldbank.org/en/837721522762050108/Environmental-and-Social-Framework.pdf) as the source for these prompts. This review version requires no new literature sweep or practice-level effect coefficients.

## How to use the prompts

1. Describe one proposed activity and the site or season under consideration. Use existing maps, reports and project records to identify plausible affected users and resources. Cite the source and note geographic or seasonal gaps.
2. Ask what could change during construction, establishment and use. Use ESS1 for alternatives and avoidance, ESS2–4 for worker safety, resource use, pollution and community safety, ESS5–8 for land access, biodiversity, Indigenous Peoples and heritage, and ESS10 for engagement and grievance handling. Ask about ESS9 where financing involves a financial intermediary.
3. Identify a design or location that could avoid harm. Record the outstanding question, who should be consulted, who owns follow-up and when it is needed. An unresolved question is a complete desk-screening output. If the proposal advances, country counterparts and the borrower can plan local verification with affected groups during preparation.
4. Carry the open question into the Stage 7 brief alongside the separate Stage 4.4 hazard shortlist choice. The TTL can narrow a shortlist. The borrower assesses impacts and manages them during preparation and implementation. The Bank undertakes due diligence and determines the formal project risk classification.

## Worked example and interpretation

Fencing young trees could block seasonal grazing access. The district team would verify the route with herders and land users and compare layouts before selecting a site. This illustrates a question and a possible alternative. Site evidence is needed to establish whether the route exists or whether the alternative works.

Mapped customary use prompts a desk flag for subsequent review. A blank map leaves the question open. Where an activity could affect peoples covered by ESS7, specialists should assess the circumstances in paragraph 24 (printed pp. 79–80), including adverse effects on customary lands or resources, relocation, or significant effects on cultural heritage integral to identity. Record an open referral until the circumstances have been established. The existing automatic FPIC wording in M2b, M3 and M6 requires separate methodological reconciliation and safeguards review.

Stage 4.3 flags a plausible concern from desk evidence. Stage 7 carries the authored example into the displayed and copied brief, distinguishing feasibility (verify the route with users), design (agree a layout and responsibilities), and implementation (monitor actual access, receive concerns and correct problems). Local checks carry no suitability or priority weight. Reviewers should assess whether the questions identify an affected party, an avoidance option and an actionable follow-up.
