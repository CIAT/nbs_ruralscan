4.2How to read Figure 4.1

  1. A hotspot is an intersection, not a sum. A cell is a hotspot when it is in the opportunity space and its need is High or Very high. Nothing is added up or averaged.
  2. Need layers are proxies at 5 km. Correlated layers are grouped and one represents each group, so the same signal is not counted twice. The others stay visible.
  3. Residents are not beneficiaries. People counted in hotspot cells live there. Who would take part or benefit is a question for feasibility.
  4. The district view summarises. It shows where hotspots concentrate; it does not locate sites.

4.5Go deeper

4.5.1How need layers are chosenThematic group, then correlation cluster, then one representative.

Each objective brings a thematic group of candidate layers. Within the area of interest, layers that move together are clustered, and one layer represents each cluster on the map. The other members of the cluster stay listed beside it, so a reader can see what the representative stands for and swap it.

4.5.2Why national data cannot rank districtsA single country value is the same everywhere on the map.

Indicators reported once per country — such as a gender inequality index — describe the setting the project works in. Applied to the map they give every district the same value, so they cannot show where need is higher. They are reported as context, and a district-level source is flagged for feasibility.
